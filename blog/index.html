Trust: The Hidden Cost of Security

Cybersecurity · Faith · Trust

The Aikido Security article “Tyro’s CISO: Being the ‘Einstein of cybersecurity’ isn’t enough if developers don’t trust you” brings up several great points. It does not matter if you are the smartest person in the room, have the best idea, or are technically right. If the team does not trust you, or if you have already burned those bridges, you will not be able to implement much of anything.

This is particularly true in cybersecurity because we are often seen as the department of “no.” With advances in AI, supply-chain attacks, and the general skill of our adversaries, we need teams to bring security into the conversation early. That will not happen if they believe we are only there to stop their work.

One point from Tyro CISO Arun Singh stood out to me. For years, security teams taught developers to keep their packages and dependencies current. Attackers have now learned to exploit that trusted behavior. As Singh put it, “threat actors have used that teaching.” The old advice was not foolish, but following it without awareness can now create another kind of risk. Aikido’s article explains why Singh’s team now recommends a cooldown period for new package versions.

That hit me in my faith. This is how I become a Pharisee. The point is not that the Pharisees were cartoon villains with no concern for God. The warning is what can happen when something intended to help us honor God becomes the main thing. Jesus said, “You have disregarded the commandment of God to keep the tradition of men” (Mark 7:8, BSB).

I can take something God gave me and focus on the gift instead of the Giver, or the promise instead of the Promiser. The practice becomes the goal, and eventually I stop paying attention to the One the practice was supposed to lead me toward.

Patching was never the root of security. It was one way to keep security in the foreground and help people remain aware. Now that attackers are exploiting the reflex to update immediately, the root has not changed. We still need procedures that put security first, but we also need people who understand their surroundings, know why those procedures exist, and are paying attention to what they are doing.

When we make a particular solution the end goal, we can lose real security. Tools, procedures, and implementations should support a security mindset; they cannot replace judgment, awareness, and trust.

Security begins with trust inside the organization. We need enough trust that when security says, “We are switching platforms,” the team can say, “I may not understand everything yet, but I know there is a reason. I know you are for us, so I will hear you out.”

Let’s keep the main thing the main thing. A security mindset is the focus. The procedures, software, and implementations are how we get there.

Source and further reading: Nicholas Thomson, “Tyro’s CISO: Being the ‘Einstein of cybersecurity’ isn’t enough if developers don’t trust you”, Aikido Security, July 21, 2026. The article is based on a conversation with Tyro CISO Arun Singh on The Secure Disclosure podcast.
