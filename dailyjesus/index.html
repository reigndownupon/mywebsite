Do You Love Me?

Day 4 · July 29, 2026

Scripture: John 21:1–19

1 After these things, Jesus revealed himself again to the disciples at the sea of Tiberias. He revealed himself this way. 2 Simon Peter, Thomas called Didymus, Nathanael of Cana in Galilee, and the sons of Zebedee, and two others of his disciples were together.

3 Simon Peter said to them, “I’m going fishing.” They told him, “We are also coming with you.” They immediately went out, and entered into the boat. That night, they caught nothing.

4 But when day had already come, Jesus stood on the beach, yet the disciples didn’t know that it was Jesus. 5 Jesus therefore said to them, “Children, have you anything to eat?” They answered him, “No.”

6 He said to them, “Cast the net on the right side of the boat, and you will find some.” They cast it therefore, and now they weren’t able to draw it in for the multitude of fish.

7 That disciple therefore whom Jesus loved said to Peter, “It’s the Lord!” So when Simon Peter heard that it was the Lord, he wrapped his coat around him (for he was naked), and threw himself into the sea. 8 But the other disciples came in the little boat (for they were not far from the land, but about two hundred cubits away), dragging the net full of fish.

9 So when they got out on the land, they saw a fire of coals there, and fish laid on it, and bread. 10 Jesus said to them, “Bring some of the fish which you have just caught.”

11 Simon Peter went up, and drew the net to land, full of great fish, one hundred fifty-three; and even though there were so many, the net wasn’t torn.

12 Jesus said to them, “Come and eat breakfast.” None of the disciples dared inquire of him, “Who are you?” knowing that it was the Lord. 13 Then Jesus came and took the bread, gave it to them, and the fish likewise. 14 This is now the third time that Jesus was revealed to his disciples, after he had risen from the dead.

15 So when they had eaten their breakfast, Jesus said to Simon Peter, “Simon, son of Jonah, do you love me more than these?” He said to him, “Yes, Lord; you know that I have affection for you.” He said to him, “Feed my lambs.”

16 He said to him again a second time, “Simon, son of Jonah, do you love me?” He said to him, “Yes, Lord; you know that I have affection for you.” He said to him, “Tend my sheep.”

17 He said to him the third time, “Simon, son of Jonah, do you have affection for me?” Peter was grieved because he asked him the third time, “Do you have affection for me?” He said to him, “Lord, you know everything. You know that I have affection for you.” Jesus said to him, “Feed my sheep.”

18 “Most certainly I tell you, when you were young, you dressed yourself, and walked where you wanted to. But when you are old, you will stretch out your hands, and another will dress you, and carry you where you don’t want to go.” 19 Now he said this, signifying by what kind of death he would glorify God. When he had said this, he said to him, “Follow me.”

John 21:1–19, WEB · Public domain

Description: After his resurrection, Jesus meets Peter and the other disciples beside the water. They have spent the night fishing and caught nothing. Jesus fills their nets, prepares breakfast, and feeds them. Only then does he turn to Peter, ask three times whether Peter loves him, entrust people to his care, and call him to follow again.

Reflection: Peter had denied Jesus three times while standing beside a charcoal fire. John now places him beside another charcoal fire, eating breakfast with the Jesus he abandoned.

The three questions naturally bring Peter’s three denials back into the room, but Jesus does not begin by demanding an explanation. He provides what Peter needs. He gives him breakfast. Peter’s failure is not ignored, but neither is Peter reduced to the worst thing he has done.

Here’s the thing: Jesus does not ask Peter to prove that he will never fail again. Peter finally has to admit that Jesus already knows him completely: “Lord, you know everything.” Jesus knows the failure, the love, the fear, and the man Peter can still become.

Then Jesus gives him people to care for.

Peter’s calling is not restored so he can recover his position or prove that he still matters. Loving Jesus must take shape in tending Jesus’ sheep. Peter is welcomed back, trusted with responsibility, and warned that following will eventually take him somewhere he would not choose for himself.

This does not mean failure has no consequences or that every past position must be returned. Jesus does not return Peter to a life untouched by his denial. He meets Peter honestly and calls him forward into costly care.

Grace does not pretend the failure never happened. Grace meets Peter beside the fire, feeds him, restores him, and says again, “Follow me.”

What this brings up for me

I see this in so many things. I have always been my own harshest critic. No one holds a grudge against me the way I hold one against myself. I remember every loss, every time I should have won, every time I did not give my all, and all of my biggest failures.

I am afraid that sins I committed while following Jesus have disqualified me from serving him.

The obvious answer to who Jesus has given me to care for is my family: my wife, my kids, my parents, and my brothers. But it is also my workplace.

I want people to know that it is not perfect people who follow Jesus. It is people who have seen how amazing Jesus is and want to share him with others.

Takeaways

  • I may hold a grudge against myself long after everyone else has moved on.
  • Jesus already knows my failures. I do not need to hide them or let them become my final identity.
  • Restoration does not erase every consequence, but failure does not get the final word.
  • Jesus calls me to care for the people in front of me, beginning with my family and extending into my workplace.
  • Following Jesus is not about being perfect. It is about seeing how amazing Jesus is and pointing others toward him.

Rich Toward God

Day 3 · July 28, 2026

Scripture: Luke 12:13–34

13 One of the multitude said to him, “Teacher, tell my brother to divide the inheritance with me.” 14 But he said to him, “Man, who made me a judge or an arbitrator over you?” 15 He said to them, “Beware! Keep yourselves from covetousness, for a man’s life doesn’t consist of the abundance of the things which he possesses.”

16 He spoke a parable to them, saying, “The ground of a certain rich man produced abundantly. 17 He reasoned within himself, saying, ‘What will I do, because I don’t have room to store my crops?’ 18 He said, ‘This is what I will do. I will pull down my barns, and build bigger ones, and there I will store all my grain and my goods. 19 I will tell my soul, “Soul, you have many goods laid up for many years. Take your ease, eat, drink, be merry.”’

20 “But God said to him, ‘You foolish one, tonight your soul is required of you. The things which you have prepared—whose will they be?’ 21 So is he who lays up treasure for himself, and is not rich toward God.”

22 He said to his disciples, “Therefore I tell you, don’t be anxious for your life, what you will eat, nor yet for your body, what you will wear. 23 Life is more than food, and the body is more than clothing. 24 Consider the ravens: they don’t sow, they don’t reap, they have no warehouse or barn, and God feeds them. How much more valuable are you than birds!

25 Which of you by being anxious can add a cubit to his height? 26 If then you aren’t able to do even the least things, why are you anxious about the rest?

27 Consider the lilies, how they grow. They don’t toil, neither do they spin; yet I tell you, even Solomon in all his glory was not arrayed like one of these. 28 But if this is how God clothes the grass in the field, which today exists, and tomorrow is cast into the oven, how much more will he clothe you, O you of little faith?

29 Don’t seek what you will eat or what you will drink; neither be anxious. 30 For the nations of the world seek after all of these things, but your Father knows that you need these things. 31 But seek God’s Kingdom, and all these things will be added to you.

32 Don’t be afraid, little flock, for it is your Father’s good pleasure to give you the Kingdom. 33 Sell that which you have, and give gifts to the needy. Make for yourselves purses which don’t grow old, a treasure in the heavens that doesn’t fail, where no thief approaches, neither moth destroys. 34 For where your treasure is, there will your heart be also.”

Luke 12:13–34, WEB · Public domain

Description: Jesus responds to a fight over money by warning that possessions cannot secure our lives. He tells a story about a man who builds bigger barns, then teaches his disciples not to let fear of scarcity rule them either. The Father knows what they need, so they can seek his kingdom and become generous instead of being controlled by accumulation or anxiety.

Reflection: This passage will not let us turn “Don’t be afraid, little flock” into a promise that God will give us more stuff or make our plans work out.

It begins with someone asking Jesus to settle a disagreement over an inheritance. Jesus warns that life is not measured by what we possess, then tells a story about a man who has more than enough but still believes bigger barns will make him secure. His plans are entirely focused on himself, and none of it can guarantee tomorrow.

Here’s the thing: greed and worry may look different, but both can keep money and possessions at the center. Greed says, “If I can get more, I will finally be safe.” Worry says, “I cannot loosen my grip because there may not be enough.”

Jesus does not pretend our needs are imaginary. He says the Father knows them. But trusting the Father is not merely reassuring ourselves that everything will work out the way we want. That trust changes what we seek, what we treasure, and what we do with what we have.

Jesus moves from bigger barns to being rich toward God, from worry to seeking the kingdom, and from fear to generosity. The question is not simply, “Do I believe God will give me what I want?” The question is, “What has my fear taught me to treasure, and will I let the kingdom reorder it?”

What this brings up for me

I am the brother, and I am the disciple. I am fighting for control over what I have while asking Jesus to make it fair. My focus stays on me, on my needs and wants, instead of turning toward God, asking what his will is for my life, and remembering how he has provided for me.

For me, becoming rich toward God means getting back to serving in the church, using the gifts God has given me to lead the next generation, and giving financially even when things are tough.

Takeaways

  • Greed and worry may look different, but both can turn my focus inward.
  • Remembering how God has provided helps move my attention from my will to his.
  • Becoming rich toward God means doing something with what God has given me.
  • Serving and giving are responses to God’s generosity, not ways of earning his care.

Learning to Follow

Day 2 · July 27, 2026
“Jesus asked him, ‘What do you want me to do for you?’ The blind man said to him, ‘Rabboni, that I may see again.’” Mark 10:51, WEB

Description: Bartimaeus was a blind beggar sitting beside the road. When Jesus passed by, he cried out for mercy. Jesus stopped and asked what he wanted. Bartimaeus answered honestly, threw off his cloak, came to Jesus, and ultimately followed him on the way.

Reflection: Bartimaeus did not make a noble-sounding request. He asked Jesus to meet a plain, physical need: he wanted to see. Jesus did not shame him for that.

The challenge was not simply what Bartimaeus asked for. It was whether he would get up, throw aside what he had been holding, and follow when Jesus called him.

Sometimes the thing I need to throw off is control. I want to make the decisions, control the outcome, and avoid losing. But that is not following. Following Jesus means trusting him when obedience costs me something and when I cannot guarantee what will happen next.

Immediately before meeting Bartimaeus, Jesus said that he came not to be served, but to serve. Following him can look ordinary: a towel, a tired night, or quietly caring for someone without keeping score.

What this brings up for me

If Jesus asked what I wanted him to do for me, my honest answer might sound selfish. I might ask him to take away my debts or make me fit. I would like to think I would ask for something noble, but I am not sure I know what I would leave behind if he called me to follow.

The first thing would have to be control. I want to control everything. I want to be the one making the decisions. That is not following.

Games are part of this too. I play too many of them, and I play too competitively. I hate to lose. That desire to win may come from the same place as my desire for control.

This week, following Jesus looks like giving my wife a rest. My sleep is not more important than hers. I can take the nighttime wake-ups and trust God with what I cannot control. I do not need to make this heroic, keep score, or use it to prove something. It can simply be love.

When I am exhausted at 3 a.m., I want to remember:

“Man shall not live by bread alone, but by every word that proceeds out of God’s mouth.” Matthew 4:4, WEB

Sleep is a real need, and I should not ignore genuine limits or unsafe exhaustion. But the natural thing is not the only thing sustaining me. God can meet me in that moment through his word and help me choose love.

Takeaways

  • Jesus welcomes honest requests, even when they do not sound noble.
  • Following requires me to release control rather than merely ask Jesus to improve my plans.
  • My need to win and my desire to control may come from the same place.
  • Serving my wife this week can be a concrete act of following Jesus.
  • Sacrifice should be quiet love, not performance or scorekeeping.
  • God’s word can sustain me when obedience costs something.
  • Trusting God does not mean ignoring real or unsafe physical limits.

Peacemakers

Day 1 · July 26, 2026
“Blessed are the peacemakers, for they will be called sons of God.” Matthew 5:9, BSB

Description: Jesus blesses people who do more than want peace. They step into tension and help make peace possible.

Reflection: Jesus does not ask us to pretend conflict is not there. He calls us to step toward it differently. Peacemaking may mean listening before defending ourselves, telling the truth without trying to wound, or taking the first step toward repair when we cannot control the other person’s response. Peace is not weakness. It is choosing to reflect God in the middle of tension.

What this brings up for me

There is always a struggle in desiring more money and thinking it will fix the problems. I tell myself that if I just had a little more, we would be okay. But this tension creates stress and strife. Instead of focusing on the here and now, I stay focused on the need for more. I wish this tension would fix itself, but it does not.

This is hard for me because many of us have been taught to keep the peace—to appease others or become people pleasers. But that is not what we are called to do. We are called to be peacemakers. It is difficult to imagine what making peace looks like instead of simply keeping it, especially when we do not control most of the variables in our lives.

Surrender is the first word that comes to mind. I need to surrender to the fact that this is what God has called me to. I am not responsible for controlling the outcome. I am responsible for obedience.

Takeaways

  • The constant desire for more can pull me away from the life God has placed in front of me.
  • Keeping the peace through people-pleasing is not the same as making peace.
  • Peacemaking requires truth, courage, and surrender.
  • God has called me to obedience, not to control the outcome.
